Ambiguity Risk in Energy Contracts

Certainty, case law, and cross-border drafting between Common Law and Civil Law

Lesson Objectives

By the end of this course, you will be able to:

  • Explain why English courts prioritise certainty in contractual interpretation, and recognise this reasoning in real case law.
  • Contrast the drafting philosophies of Common Law (England) and Civil Law (Italy), including buona fede and culpa in contrahendo.
  • Distinguish patent and latent ambiguity, and identify where each typically arises in energy contracts.
  • Use precise, advanced legal vocabulary relating to force majeure, exclusion clauses, and dispute resolution.
  • Draft — and defend, in writing — a clearer version of a genuinely ambiguous clause.
Before you begin — reflect on:
  • Have you ever seen a contract clause interpreted in a way you didn't expect? What made it ambiguous?
  • Do you think a contract should say everything explicitly, or rely on general principles of fairness?
  • What comes to mind when you hear the phrase "reasonable endeavours"?

Energy contracts sit at the intersection of two pressures that rarely pull in the same direction: commercial volatility, which demands flexibility, and legal certainty, which demands precision. When a clause can reasonably be read in more than one way, that tension turns into a dispute, and in the energy sector, where a single cargo of liquefied natural gas can be worth tens of millions of dollars, ambiguity is rarely a minor drafting slip. It is a live commercial risk. This course examines how that risk arises, how English courts resolve it, and how the underlying legal culture of a contract – common law or civil law – shapes the drafting choices that prevent it.

A. Why Certainty Matters: English Law's Approach to Interpretation

Click play to listen

English commercial law has, for centuries, prized one value above almost all others: certainty. Judges repeatedly explain that commercial parties need to know, in advance, exactly where they stand, so that they can price risk and plan their affairs accordingly. This is why English courts lean so heavily on the natural and ordinary meaning of the words a contract actually uses, rather than asking what might seem fair or reasonable with hindsight. Four recent cases illustrate the point. In RTI v MUR Shipping, the Supreme Court held that a force majeure clause requiring "reasonable endeavours" did not force a shipowner to accept payment in a different currency than the contract specified – accepting non-contractual performance is a choice, not an obligation, however reasonable it might seem. In Abbey Healthcare v Augusta, the Court had to decide whether a collateral warranty signed after building works were finished counted as a "construction contract" for statutory purposes; the Supreme Court drew a bright line to avoid case-by-case uncertainty, even though the Court of Appeal below had reached the opposite conclusion using the same language of "certainty". In Providence v Hexagon, a termination clause was read broadly because a narrow reading would have left a contractor with only slow, costly remedies for repeated late payment. And in Morganstone v Birkemp, a payment schedule that had simply run out of dates did not leave the parties in a vacuum, because the underlying contractual mechanism still worked perfectly well without it. The common thread is this: English judges will not rewrite a bad bargain to rescue a party from it, but they will work hard to find the one meaning that best serves commercial certainty.

Case spotlight: In RTI Ltd v MUR Shipping BV (2024), the UK Supreme Court held that a shipowner facing a force majeure event was not obliged to accept payment in a non-contractual currency, even though doing so might have overcome the problem — "reasonable endeavours" does not require accepting non-contractual performance.

B. Two Legal Traditions, Two Drafting Philosophies

Click play to listen

Why does English law behave this way? Because it belongs to a common law tradition that treats the written contract as a largely self-contained universe. Since there is no comprehensive civil code filling in the gaps, English contracts tend to be long and exhaustive, spelling out every scenario the parties can imagine, because anything left unsaid may simply not exist as an obligation. English law also rejects any broad, freestanding duty of good faith during negotiations: a party is generally free to walk away from talks at any point before a binding contract is signed, no matter how advanced those talks were. Italian law, rooted in the Civil Code, works differently. Because the Codice Civile automatically supplies default rules, definitions and statutory protections, Italian contracts can afford to be shorter. Italian law also imposes buona fede, an overarching duty of good faith, both during negotiations and during performance; breaking off negotiations abruptly, or negotiating with several counterparts in parallel without disclosure, can trigger liability for culpa in contrahendo even before any contract exists. Italian judges, moreover, have more latitude to look past the literal wording to the parties' real intent, or causa. For a lawyer moving between these systems, the practical lesson is this: a clause that would be read generously, in light of fairness, under Italian law may be read with unforgiving literalism under English law. Precision is not a stylistic preference in English-law contracts; it is the entire safety net.

Key contrast: Italian law's buona fede creates real pre-contractual exposure through culpa in contrahendo; English law's freedom to negotiate (and to walk away) is close to absolute until a binding contract exists.

C. Ambiguity in Energy Contracts: Where Disputes Begin

Click play to listen

Contract ambiguity is not one single problem. Lawyers distinguish between patent ambiguity, which is visible on the face of the document – two clauses that flatly contradict each other – and latent ambiguity, which stays invisible until the contract meets a real situation it was never quite designed for. A phrase like "delivery within a reasonable time" looks perfectly clear until the parties are arguing, months later, over whether three weeks was reasonable for a particular cargo in a particular season. Latent ambiguity is the more dangerous of the two precisely because nobody notices it until it is expensive. The energy sector manufactures latent ambiguity at scale, because so many of its obligations depend on conditions that shift after signature. Grid connection queues lengthen, non-commodity costs float, metering data lags behind actual usage, and supply chains for transformers or turbine blades can fail without warning. A force majeure clause built around vague phrases such as "supply chain disruption" or "unforeseen market shifts" leaves enormous room for dispute over whether a grid outage or a regulatory shock genuinely excuses performance. Equally, a volumetric commitment expressed as "reasonable endeavours to deliver" or "seasonal adjustments", without numerical parameters, hands the final word to whichever tribunal ends up interpreting it after the fact. Retroactive "back-billing", triggered by metering lag, is a further recurring flashpoint: a distributor issues an invoice covering months of previously unmetered usage, and the buyer discovers that the contract never made clear how far back such billing could reach. None of this is inevitable. Courts routinely rely on tools such as contra proferentem – interpreting ambiguity against whichever party drafted the clause – or on the parties' course of performance, but these are remedies for a drafting failure, not a substitute for precision at the outset.

Patent vs. latent, in one line: a patent ambiguity is a contradiction you can see on page one; a latent ambiguity is a landmine you only find by stepping on it.

D. Landmark Disputes: LNG, Exclusion Clauses & Force Majeure

Click play to listen

Two recent English cases show how far a single word can travel once it reaches arbitration or litigation. In the long-term LNG supply disputes between Venture Global and its buyers Shell and BP, two tribunals looked at almost identical facts – cargoes sold on the spot market before a new export terminal had reached full commercial operation – and reached opposite results. The Shell tribunal focused on the contractual start date and commissioning milestones and found for the seller; the BP tribunal focused on the seller's duty to declare the commercial operation date promptly and to act as a "reasonable and prudent operator", and found partly against it. The lesson buyers are drawing is to insist on tighter, metric-based definitions of the commercial operation date and explicit limits on a seller's discretion to sell elsewhere during commissioning; sellers, in turn, are seeking broader safe-harbour carve-outs and a more precisely bounded definition of "reasonable and prudent operator" duties. In a different sector entirely, EE v Virgin Mobile shows how a single broadly worded exclusion clause can swallow an entire claim. EE sought over twenty million pounds in damages after Virgin allegedly breached exclusivity terms, but the Court of Appeal held that this loss – described by EE as "lost charges" rather than "lost profits" – still fell within an exclusion for "anticipated profits", because it was, in substance, the expectation loss the clause was written to exclude. The majority was clear: a clearly worded risk allocation, negotiated between sophisticated commercial parties, will be enforced even where it leaves one side without an effective remedy. One judge dissented, arguing the clause should not reach a breach of a core contractual obligation – a reminder that even experienced judges can disagree about where the boundary of a broad exclusion actually falls.

E. The Modern Arbitration & Regulatory Landscape

Click play to listen

The regulatory and procedural backdrop to these disputes is also shifting. The Arbitration Act 2025, which entered into force on 1 August 2025, reinforces London's position as an arbitration seat: it introduces a default rule that the arbitration agreement itself is governed by the law of the seat unless the parties expressly agree otherwise, imposes a duty on arbitrators to disclose circumstances that might raise doubts about their impartiality, and allows tribunals to dispose of hopeless claims on a summary basis. For energy contracts that name London as the seat while choosing a different governing law for the underlying contract, this default rule matters enormously in practice. At the same time, the modernised Energy Charter Treaty narrows protection for fossil-fuel investments while extending it to transition technologies such as carbon capture, and introduces a new definition of indirect expropriation designed to make clear that non-discriminatory climate measures will not, absent rare circumstances, count as expropriation. The International Court of Justice's 2025 advisory opinion that States owe binding, erga omnes obligations to prevent climate harm adds a further layer of legal risk that energy companies and their contract drafters are only beginning to price in. Taken together, these developments point in one direction: the parties who invest the most care in precise, unambiguous drafting today are the ones best placed to avoid becoming the next contested arbitration.

Applied Section: Redrafting an Ambiguous Clause

Original clause (deliberately vague): "The Seller shall use reasonable endeavours to deliver the agreed volumes within a reasonable time, subject to seasonal adjustments."

Working alone, rewrite this clause so that a tribunal applying strict English-law literalism would have almost nothing left to argue about. Consider: numerical thresholds, defined terms, and a clear allocation of the risk of delay.

Myth vs. Reality

✗ Myth: "Eventualmente" translates directly to "eventually".
✓ Reality: "Eventualmente" means possibly / if applicable in Italian legal usage; the English false friend "eventually" means in the end, after some time. Confusing the two can quietly change the meaning of a clause.
✗ Myth: A general duty of good faith applies to pre-contractual negotiations under English law, just as it does in Italy.
✓ Reality: English law rejects a broad, freestanding duty of good faith in negotiations — parties may walk away at almost any point before signature, unlike under Italian culpa in contrahendo.
✗ Myth: "Actually" means "attualmente" (currently).
✓ Reality: "Actually" means in fact / in reality; "currently" or "at present" is the correct translation of "attualmente". This false friend appears constantly in Italian legal English and can distort a client's meaning.

Exercise 8 — Opinion Piece (sample answer)

"Firstly, English law's rejection of a general duty of good faith supports certainty, since parties know precisely where negotiation ends and obligation begins. However, this can allow opportunistic withdrawal from advanced negotiations. By contrast, Italy's buona fede protects a party's reliance interest through culpa in contrahendo, at some cost to predictability. On balance, a narrow, clearly defined duty — rather than Italy's broad standard — might strike a better balance for English commercial law."

Accept any well-organised position that engages with both systems and uses at least 3 connectors correctly. Push stronger students to hedge claims ("might", "could arguably") rather than making absolute statements.
